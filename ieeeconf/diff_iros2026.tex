\begin{abstract}
Learning \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{language-conditioned }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{language-guided }\DIFaddend robot manipulation policies requires representations that capture not only the current scene but also how it changes under task instructions. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{While }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Existing }\DIFaddend general-purpose visual representations \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{have improved robotic perception, they are mainly focused on encoding static scenes and }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{typically encode the current visual scene and hence }\DIFaddend provide limited guidance on the temporal dynamics relevant to robotic actions. We propose \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{LARM, }\DIFdelend a self-supervised \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{learning }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{pre-training }\DIFaddend framework that extracts task-relevant \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{temporal dynamics }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{future changes from action-free human videos and represents them }\DIFaddend as latent residuals \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{. These intent-aware residuals provide a clear and direct target for the robot actions. }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{for downstream policy learning. }\DIFaddend During \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{pre-training, a shared future predictor operates with asymmetric visual access: both use the global visual context and instruction, while the full-information stream has additional access to dense visual tokens. The other stream, called intent stream, is guided by the full-information prediction to capture task-relevant future changes. During }\DIFaddend policy learning, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{LARM bridges the gap between pre-training and deployment by retaining the predictive module to compute an explicit latent residual between }\DIFdelend the \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{intent stream predicts the future latent state from the }\DIFaddend current observation and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{the predicted future state. This lightweight, control-oriented signal highlights the necessary actions directly, which simplifies the learning process of the policy. Experiments in both simulated and }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{instruction, and we compute its residual from the current latent state. The policy is conditioned on both the current representation and this residual, providing an explicit task-relevant change signal for action prediction. Experiments on two simulation benchmarks and seven }\DIFaddend real-robot \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{manipulation environments }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{tasks }\DIFaddend demonstrate that these latent residuals serve as an effective prior for policy learning\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{.
}%DIFDELCMD < 

%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, outperforming prior methods.
}\DIFaddend \end{abstract}

Learning robot manipulation policies fundamentally requires rich, structured representations that capture object semantics and geometry, spatial relationships, dynamics, and task-relevant affordances. However, learning such representations without extensive manual supervision remains a fundamental challenge. }%DIFDELCMD <

\DIFdel{Recent advances in self-supervised }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Learning-based robot manipulation requires visual representations that describe not only what is currently visible, but also how the scene should change for a task to succeed. While recent advances in visual }\DIFaddend representation learning have enabled \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{general-purpose models, such as DINO~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{caron2021emerging}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
, R3M~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{nair2022r3m}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
, and VIP~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{ma2022vip}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
, which provide transferrable features for }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{frozen visual features to support }\DIFaddend robotic perception and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{manipulation. These models improve robots' ability to extract semantically meaningful information from the scene}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{control, manipulation is inherently temporal, as effective control requires a policy to identify which object motion, contact event, or configuration change matters next}\DIFaddend . However, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{these representations are fundamentally static, as they are learned to characterize the current state of the scene (}\textit{\DIFdel{what is in the scene}}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{), rather than capturing temporal transition (}\textit{\DIFdel{how the world is changed}}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{). In result, downstream control policies are required to learn the capability of inferring }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{many generic representations either lack temporal knowledge or encode it only implicitly. Static features do not directly indicate how the scene is expected to evolve, forcing downstream controllers to infer }\DIFaddend task-relevant dynamics \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{and physical transitions, implicitly discovering which visual changes are critical to task completion. 
This separation between perception and dynamics understanding forces policies to re-learn, from scratch, the temporal structure that is essential for manipulation}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{from limited demonstrations}\DIFaddend ~\cite{hewage2020temporal,zakka2022xirl}. 


%DIF <  However, these representations are inherently static—they excel at describing \textit{what is}, but remain silent about \textit{how the world should change}.

%DIF <  The burden of inferring actionable dynamics and physical transitions is left entirely to downstream control policies, which must implicitly discover which visual changes matter for task completion. This separation between perception and dynamics understanding forces policies to re-learn, from scratch, the temporal structure that is essential for manipulation.
\DIFdelbegin %DIFDELCMD < 

%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdel{Learning predictive representations offers a natural solution by introducing a temporal structure through future-state forecasting. By learning to predict the future state, a model can capture the dynamics that policies need}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Predictive representation learning provides a natural way to model these temporal changes, while existing predictive pipelines do not directly employ a control-oriented change signal to the policy. Future prediction has long been used to learn dynamics useful for decision making}\DIFaddend ~\cite{hafner2019dream,finn2017deep}. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{However, existing approaches show two critical limitations. First, many approaches rely on computationally heavy pixel-level reconstructions~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{he2022masked, gupta2023siamese, zhu2025lava, zeng2024learning, karamcheti2023language,kim2025token}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
, forcing models to allocate a capacity to learn high-frequency }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{In vision-based representation learning, many methods train by reconstructing or predicting future visual content~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{he2022masked,gupta2023siamese,zhu2025lava,zeng2024learning,karamcheti2023language,kim2025token}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. Such objectives can allocate capacity to }\DIFaddend visual details that \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{might be irrelevant to operating robotic tasks. This entangles manipulation-induced object motion with passive dynamics or environmental noise. Second, existing models typically discard the predictive decoder entirely during downstream policy training~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{nair2022r3m, radosavovic2023real, xiao2022masked}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. By retaining only the static encoder (Fig. ~\ref{fig:teaser}, top), these models discard dynamics information that predictive learning is designed to capture, forcing downstream task policies to re-solve the disentanglement problem from scratch.
}%DIFDELCMD < 

%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdel{In this work, we hypothesize that predictive learning should not be treated only as a }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{are not decisive for manipulation. Moreover, the temporal component is often discarded or compressed after }\DIFaddend pre-training\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{task that will be discarded, but rather as a mechanism for extracting }\textit{\DIFdel{intent-aligned state transitions}} %DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{directly within the latent space.
Instead of predicting all future environmental changes, we focus on identifying specific future states that align with the intended task.
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{: predictive decoders may be removed before downstream learning~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{radosavovic2023real,xiao2022masked}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
, while objectives such as time-contrastive learning~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{nair2022r3m} }\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
leave temporal information only implicitly inside a static embedding. The challenge is therefore not simply how to learn future prediction, but how to retain the task-relevant part of prediction as an explicit policy input.
}\DIFaddend 

We \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{present LARM (\textbf{L}earning Intent-\textbf{A}ligned Latent Residuals for Language-Conditioned \textbf{R}obotic \textbf{M}anipulation)}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{propose TRAIL (\textbf{T}ask-\textbf{R}elevant residuals as an \textbf{A}ction prior for robot man\textbf{I}pu\textbf{L}ation)}\DIFaddend , a self-supervised \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{representation learning framework that constrains future latent representations using task semantics derived from an image and language instructions. Specifically, LARM uses two distinct prediction modules. One module has access to detailed visual features of the scene,
while }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{framework that learns a task-relevant latent change from action-free human videos}\footnote{\DIFadd{For more results: }\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/w/ICRA2027-E3D6/index.html}}\DIFadd{. TRAIL defines current and future states in a frozen visual feature space and trains a shared future predictor with asymmetric visual access. \section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Visual representations for robotic manipulation}
Vision foundation models have become widely used for robotic representation learning. Self-supervised approaches extract robust features from static observations via time-contrastive learning (e.g., R3M~\cite{nair2022r3m}, VIP~\cite{ma2022vip}, LIV~\cite{ma2023liv}) and masked autoencoders (e.g., MAE~\cite{he2022masked}, MVP~\cite{radosavovic2023real}, VC-1~\cite{majumdar2023we}). To incorporate semantic priors, vision-language models align visual features with language instructions, enabling open-vocabulary manipulation (e.g., CLIPort~\cite{shridhar2022cliport}, RT-1~\cite{brohan2022rt}). 


These methods \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{primarily }\DIFdelend produce static representations that capture \textit{what} is in the scene, but not \textit{how} it changes over time. To extract more robust and task-relevant signals, recent approaches extend these static representations using the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle~\cite{tishby2000information}. By strictly limiting information capacity, IB-based methods learn compressed representations that filter out irrelevant distractors, improving downstream reinforcement learning~\cite{you2024multimodal} and behavior cloning~\cite{bai2025rethinking}. However, these IB-based methods still lack explicit modeling of \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{task-conditioned }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{task-relevant }\DIFaddend dynamics, as they compress the current state while not predicting its future evolution.

\subsection{Predictive modeling for visual control}
An alternative approach is learning representations by predicting future observations, which effectively captures environment dynamics. Several approaches focus on pixel-level video prediction (e.g., RoboNet~\cite{dasari2019robonet}, UniPi~\cite{du2023learning}, SUSIE~\cite{black2023zero}), for visual planning or sub-goal generation. Other approaches, such as Voltron~\cite{karamcheti2023language} and LaVA-Man~\cite{zhu2025lava}, extend such pixel-level prediction by integrating language and action conditioning into image reconstruction and forward video prediction. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Latent action models~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{schmidt2024learning, bruce2024genie,ye2025latent} }\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
learn the action conditioning without action labels. These models infer a low-capacity latent action from the current and future frames and use it to reconstruct the future frame's pixels from the current frame. The latent actions are then used to pre-train policies, but must later be mapped to executable actions~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{ye2025latent, agibot2025world}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. }\DIFaddend However, forecasting at the pixel level is computationally expensive and overly sensitive to task-irrelevant visual distractions such as lighting variations or background movement\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{lecun2022path,bardes2024revisiting}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFaddend .

Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs)~\cite{lecun2022path} address the limitation of pixel-level prediction by shifting the prediction objective to the latent space, which improves efficiency. I-JEPA~\cite{assran2023self} and V-JEPA~\cite{bardes2024revisiting} learn powerful spatial and spatiotemporal representations by predicting the latent embeddings of masked regions. In the robotics domain, MC-JEPA~\cite{benchetrit2024mcjepa} jointly models motion and content features, which can capture both scene appearance and dynamics. As a recent extension to predictive models, ToBo (Token Bottleneck)~\cite{kim2025token} squeezes the current scene into a compact bottleneck token to predict future latent patches, forcing the model to encode temporal dependencies. 

Despite these advances, standard latent predictive models (e.g., Siamese MAE~\cite{gupta2023siamese}) still attempt to forecast the \textit{full} future state, inevitably entangling controllable transitions with uncontrollable passive dynamics. In contrast, our proposed \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{LARM }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{TRAIL }\DIFaddend combines the efficiency of latent prediction with \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{an intent-conditioned }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{a task-relevant }\DIFaddend bottleneck. Unlike prior methods, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{LARM explicitly predicts }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{we explicitly produce }\DIFaddend the \textit{residual} between current and future states, providing policies with a gradient-like directional cue that isolates \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{task-compatible dynamics }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{task-relevant dynamics without requiring any mapping to executable actions}\DIFaddend .

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Our method leverages large-scale, general-domain human video datasets (e.g., web videos ) for self-supervised pre-training rather than relying on robot-specific trajectories. TRAIL instead constrains the \emph{visual memory access}: the intent stream retains the language-grounded visual class token and contextualized language tokens but has no direct access to dense patch tokens. This capacity limit forces compression, while language grounding determines which information is emphasized. \emph{(ii) Cross-embodiment transfer.} A latent action inferred from human hands in web video is defined relative to the human embodiment, and transferring it to a parallel-jaw gripper requires the alignment stage to absorb that mismatch. A state residual carries no embodiment: it is a direction in a perceptual space shared by both, which is what allows TRAIL to pre-train on Kinetics and deploy on a robot without any in-domain robot data. \emph{(iii) How dynamics are learned without latent-action supervision.} The supervision is the prediction task itself. The model must map the current observation and the instruction onto the frozen-encoder embedding of a future frame, so $\Delta_z$ is exactly the $k$-step transition that the instruction implies. TRAIL does not need to identify \emph{which command} produced that transition, because the downstream policy is trained with real action labels; the residual only has to indicate where the scene should go, not what to send to the controller.

%DIF >  The trade-off is explicit. Because $\Delta_z$ is not an action, it cannot be rolled out for planning or used as a policy target on its own, which latent action models can do.     \DIFdel{\mathcal{L}_{\text{cons}} = \alpha \cdot \| F_{\text{intent}} - \text{sg}(F_{\text{full}}) \|^2, %DIFDELCMD <%%%
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{~\ref{eq:loss_int}, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{full}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}$ measure the prediction errors of the full-information and intent streams with respect to $z^{\mathrm{gt}}_{t+k}$. In Eq.~\ref{eq:loss_cons}, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}$ encourages the intent prediction to agree with the patch-informed full-information prediction, while $\operatorname{sg}(\cdot)$ stops gradients through the full-information output. The overall pre-training loss becomes
%DIF > 
}\begin{equation}
\DIFadd{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}
=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{full}}
+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}
+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}},
\label{eq:total}
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend 
%DIF > 
where \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\text{sg}(\cdot)$ denotes a stop-gradient operator, which prevents gradients from propagating through $F_{\text{full}}$, and $\alpha=0.3$ is a hyperparameter to scale the effect of the loss. This encourages the intent-aligned stream to agree with }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\alpha$ weights the consistency loss, empirically set to $\alpha=0.3$. 
Since $\operatorname{sg}(\cdot)$ is applied to }\DIFaddend the full-information \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{stream while maintaining independence. Since $F_{\text{intent}}$ operates under an information bottleneck, minimizing this consistency loss effectively filters $F_{\text{full}}$'s predictions: futures that cannot be predicted from task context alone are suppressed. The model thus learns to allocate capacity primarily to intent-aligned dynamics. The total objective combines Eq.~\ref{eq:loss_full}, Eq.~\ref{eq:loss_intent}, and Eq.
~\ref{eq:consistency}:
}\begin{displaymath}
    \DIFdel{\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{full}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{intent}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{cons}}. %DIFDELCMD <%%%
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{prediction, gradients from $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}$ propagate only through the intent branch. The future-frame visual encoder and language encoder are frozen, while the current-frame visual encoder, language projection, fusion module, future queries, and asymmetric predictor are trainable.
}\DIFaddend 



\subsection{Residual-guided policy learning}
\label{subsec:policy_learning}


\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Following }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{After }\DIFaddend pre-training, we \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{adapt the learned representations for downstream manipulation. Existing approaches~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{black2023zero,zhu2025lava} }\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
condition policies on fully reconstructed future images, which introduces significant computational overhead and generates redundant static information, e.g. unchanged static background. Therefore, we discard the full predictor and retain only $F_{\text{intent}}$, now denoted as $z_{t+1}$, to compute a latent residual
:
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{keep only the intent stream and use its predicted future latent to construct a task-relevant residual for downstream policy learning. Given the current observation and instruction, the intent stream produces $\hat z^{\mathrm{int}}_{t+k}$ in the same frozen visual feature space as $z_t$. We then form the token-wise residual
}\DIFaddend %
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_z = \DIFaddbegin \hat \DIFaddend z\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{_{t+1} - z}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{^{\mathrm{int}}_{t+k}-z}\DIFaddend _t\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, }\DIFaddend \label{eq:residual}
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{.
}\DIFdelend \end{equation}
%DIF < 
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{The residual }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{where }\DIFaddend $\Delta_z$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{in Eq.~\ref{eq:residual} represents the minimal update between current and future states. In other words, $\Delta_z$ can be considered as a directional cue that highlights what should change to achieve the task. Rather than providing the policy with a full future state, which forces the policy network to implicitly compute the }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{represents the task-relevant change from the current latent state. Unlike an absolute future feature, this representation makes the }\DIFaddend difference between current and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{future observations to deduce the required motion}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{predicted states explicit rather than leaving the policy to infer that difference implicitly}\DIFaddend .

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{To translate this latent residual into motor commands, we condition the policy on both the current state $z_t$ and the predicted change $\Delta_z$. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:acdino}, }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{As }\DIFaddend $\Delta_z$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{is first fused with the current state $z_t$ via SNR-enhanced normalization. This operation amplifies the $\Delta_z$ signal, ensuring that it is not disregarded as background noise by the policy model, thus producing a combined representation $z_{fused} = \text{Norm}(z_t \oplus \Delta_z)$. This representation is then concatenated with proprioceptive joint states $q_t$ and fed into the policy network $\pi(a_t | z_{fused}, q_t)$, which outputs actions via standard behavioral cloning or reinforcement learning objectives}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{can have a smaller magnitude than the current-state features, we normalize the two token sequences independently before passing them to the downstream policy. This prevents their scale difference from suppressing the residual during feature fusion. We then train the downstream policy by imitation learning using action-labeled robot demonstrations. During downstream policy learning, the pre-trained visual encoder, language-fusion module, and intent predictor are kept frozen, while only the action decoder is trained}\DIFaddend .


\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{The latent residual $\Delta_z$ can be adapted to different policy architectures depending on the action space. For }\textit{\DIFdel{1D continuous control}} %DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{that uses state-based algorithms predicting joint angles or end-effector poses~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{assran2025v}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
, we flatten the }\texttt{%DIFDELCMD < [%%%
\DIFdel{CLS}%DIFDELCMD < ]%%%
} %DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{residual into a 1D vector and concatenate it with $q_t$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{TRAIL can be integrated with different downstream policy architectures through the same residual representation. For simulation benchmarks, e.g. Franka Kitchen and Meta-World, we concatenate $z_t$ and $\Delta_z$ with proprioception use them }\DIFaddend as input to \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) policy. In }\textit{\DIFdel{2D spatial-action policies}} %DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{designed for vision-based affordance models  (e.g., tabletop }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{an action decoder that outputs continuous control commands. For real-robot tabletop tasks, we reshape the patch tokens from both $z_t$ and $\Delta_z$ into spatial feature maps and fuse them to predict language-conditioned affordance maps~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{shridhar2022cliport}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. Thus, the same pre-trained TRAIL representations can be used across different action spaces by adapting only the downstream readout.
}

\DIFadd{At deployment, the intent stream is executed at every control step in closed loop. Given the current image and instruction, the model predicts $\hat z^{\mathrm{int}}_{t+k}$ and computes $\Delta_z$, and then predicts an action. This process is repeated after each robot action, and the predictor is not rolled out over multiple future steps. Here a control step denotes one policy decision, whose granularity follows the action space. For the continuous-control benchmarks, one decision is a single low-level command, so the predictor is re-evaluated at the environment control frequency. For the tabletop affordance read-out, one decision is a complete }\DIFaddend pick-and-place \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{shridhar2022cliport}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
), we extract the residual of patch tokens , reshape it into a 2D spatial feature map, and fuse it with the policy's visual backbone via convolutional layers, providing an explicit spatial prior on where and how to interact. This flexibility allows LARM to integrate seamlessly with both low-dimensional state policies and high-dimensional visuomotor architectures without architectural modification. }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{primitive: a single prediction supplies both endpoints of the motion from one observation, and the primitive is executed without re-observation before the next decision is made. 
Note that, during pre-training, $k$ specifies the frame gap between $I_t$ and $I_{t+k}$ and does not determine the robot action frequency. The downstream policy instead learns the action timescale from labeled demonstrations.
}\DIFaddend

\noindent\textbf{Pre-training details.} 
We pre-train \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{LARM strictly }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{TRAIL }\DIFaddend on human-centric videos using the Kinetics-400 training split~\cite{kay2017kinetics, carreira2017quo}, without any in-domain robotic manipulation data. To construct the dataset for our prediction-based self-supervised learning, we sample pairs of frames from each video. Specifically, we select the $1^{\text{st}}$ frame as the initial observation and the $96^{\text{th}}$ frame as the target future state. This specific temporal gap ($k=96$) is chosen to capture meaningful, long-horizon state transitions rather than trivial, high-frequency motions\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, as in~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{kim2025token}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{~\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{kim2025token}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. Kinetics-400 is stored at $25$--$30$\,fps, so $k=96$ spans roughly $3$--$4$ seconds of human activity. The gap is large enough so that the transition is not dominated by encoding noise. $k$ is a hyper-parameter of pre-training on web video and }\emph{\DIFadd{not}} \DIFadd{a control horizon, i.e. at deployment the predictor is executed at every control step and its output is consumed as a direction, with magnitude and time scale by the action decoder (Sec.~\ref{subsec:policy_learning})}\DIFaddend .

\section{Conclusion}
We proposed \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{LARM, a prediction-based }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{TRAIL, a }\DIFaddend self-supervised \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{representation learning framework }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{learning framework that learns predictive representations }\DIFaddend for robot manipulation policies. The key idea is to estimate \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{intent-aware latent residuals that encourage the model to focus on task-relevant changes, }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{task-relevant latent residuals }\DIFaddend and then use these \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{latent }\DIFdelend residuals for effective policy learning. Simulation and real-robot experiments demonstrate that our model benefits the learning for both continuous control and tabletop arrangement policies, outperforming prior methods. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{In the future, we }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Future work }\DIFaddend will extend our framework to more diverse manipulation tasks and real-world robotic settings.
