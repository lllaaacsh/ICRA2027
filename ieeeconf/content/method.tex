\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

 Our method leverages large-scale, general-domain human video datasets (e.g., web videos) for self-supervised pre-training rather than relying on robot-specific trajectories. This allows the model to learn generalized motion priors by understanding the correlation between visual dynamics and language-based action annotations in datasets like Kinetics~\cite{kay2017kinetics} (e.g., ``a person is water sliding''). By capturing the underlying structure of these motions, the model develops a robust understanding of physical dynamics, which can then be transferred to robotic control with minimal post-training. To this end, we formulate a two-stage framework: (1) \textit{self-supervised pre-training with intent-aligned prediction}, which learns to isolate task-relevant dynamics by predicting future latent states under an information bottleneck (Sec.~\ref{subsec:encoder} and ~\ref{subsec:pretraing}), and (2) \textit{residual-guided policy learning}, which conditions the downstream policy on the predicted change between current and future states (Sec.~\ref{subsec:policy_learning}).

\subsection{Language-grounded visual encoding}
\label{subsec:encoder}
In Fig.~\ref{fig:ab_probe}, we show the model architecture for our self-supervised pre-training. The pre-training begins by grounding visual features in natural language descriptions of general human activities. Inspired by GLIP~\cite{li2022grounded} and recent vision-language fusion techniques for robotics~\cite{karamcheti2023language}, we employ bidirectional cross-attention to fuse information from the two modalities. This fusion enhances the semantic consistency between task intent and scene features, so that task-relevant information is compressed into the global context representation, i.e.\ the \rev{visual} \texttt{[CLS]} token.

{\color{revcolor}
\noindent\textbf{Notation.}
A frozen DINOv3 backbone~\cite{simeoni2025dinov3} maps the observation $I_t$ to a token sequence $V = [\,v_{\texttt{cls}}, v_1, \dots, v_N\,] \in \mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times d}$, where $v_{\texttt{cls}}$ is the visual class token and $N=196$ is the number of $16\times16$ patches at $224\times224$ resolution. A frozen DistilBERT~\cite{sanh2019distilbert} maps the instruction to its full token sequence $L = [\,\ell_1, \dots, \ell_M\,]$, which we linearly project to the visual width. Throughout this paper \texttt{[CLS]} refers \emph{exclusively} to the visual class token; the special tokens of the language encoder are never used as separate inputs.
}

Given $V$ and $L$, we fuse the two modalities through two bidirectional cross-attention layers:
%
\begin{align}
    V' &= \texttt{CrossAttn}(V, L, L) + V \label{eq:vision_update}, \\
    L' &= \texttt{CrossAttn}(L, V', V') + L \label{eq:language_update},
\end{align}
%
where the first, second and third arguments of $\texttt{CrossAttn}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)$ are queries, keys and values, respectively.
As shown in Eq.~\ref{eq:vision_update} and Eq.~\ref{eq:language_update}, these layers have two effects: visual patches are conditioned on the language context (attention shifts to regions mentioned in the instruction), while language tokens are contextualised by the current scene layout (the same instruction yields different grounding depending on object locations). \rev{The resulting $v'_{\texttt{cls}}$ therefore serves as a semantic anchor that summarises both scene content and task intent in a \emph{single} $d$-dimensional vector, while the updated patch embeddings $v'_1,\dots,v'_N$ preserve dense, localised spatial evidence. The asymmetry exploited in the next section is precisely the gap between these two levels of description.}

\subsection{Asymmetric intent-aligned prediction}
\label{subsec:pretraing}

{\color{revcolor}
This subsection trains a deployable predictor for task-conditioned future latents rather than generic video extrapolation. A predictor with dense access to the current frame can model future tokens from local appearance and passive scene motion, but a robot policy needs the part of that future that is implied by the instruction. LARM therefore evaluates one shared predictor under two access conditions: a full-information pass that can read dense visual memory, and an intent-aligned pass that must forecast from language-grounded global context. The full pass provides a memory-conditioned training reference, while the intent pass is the one retained for downstream control.

\noindent\textbf{Prediction target.}
Given the current frame $I_t$ and instruction $c$, the pre-training target is the frozen-teacher representation of the frame $k$ steps later,
$z^{\text{gt}}_{t+k}=[\,g_{\texttt{cls}},g_1,\dots,g_N\,]$.
The offset $k$ is a web-video pre-training gap, not a robot control horizon. The DINOv3 backbone that produces the current tokens $z_t=[\,v_{\texttt{cls}},v_1,\dots,v_N\,]$ is also frozen, so $z_t$ and $z^{\text{gt}}_{t+k}$ are defined in the same feature space and token-wise residuals are meaningful.

\noindent\textbf{One predictor, two access conditions.}
The two streams are not two networks; they are two calls to the same predictor $F_\theta$ with different memory sets. $F_\theta$ is a stack of decoder blocks, each containing cross-attention from query tokens to memory, query self-attention, and an MLP. Its queries $Q=[\,q_{\texttt{cls}},q_1,\dots,q_N\,]$ are learned, content-free future tokens distinguished by positional embeddings, so the queries themselves do not carry current-frame appearance. All scene information must enter through the memory $M$:
%
\begin{align}
    \hat z^{\text{full}}_{t+k} &= F_\theta\big(Q \mid M_{\text{full}}\big), &
    M_{\text{full}} &= [\,v'_{\texttt{cls}},\, v'_{1:N},\, L'\,], \label{eq:stream_full}\\
    \hat z^{\text{int}}_{t+k}  &= F_\theta\big(Q \mid M_{\text{int}}\big),  &
    M_{\text{int}}  &= [\,v'_{\texttt{cls}},\, L'\,]. \label{eq:stream_int}
\end{align}
%
The full-information stream can read dense spatial evidence $v'_{1:N}$ at every decoder block. The intent-aligned stream removes that dense visual memory and keeps only the language-grounded visual class token $v'_{\texttt{cls}}$ together with the language tokens $L'$. This creates a visual bottleneck without reducing the output resolution: the intent stream still predicts $N{+}1$ future tokens because spatial slots are supplied by $Q$. Since the two calls share $\theta$ and execute the same operations, the controlled variable is memory access rather than parameter capacity.

\noindent\textbf{The instruction reaches the predictor twice.}
Language affects the forecast both through fused visual tokens and through the retained language memory. Eq.~\ref{eq:vision_update} makes $v'_{\texttt{cls}}$ a task-conditioned visual summary, which determines what scene information can survive the intent bottleneck. Eq.~\ref{eq:language_update} keeps the instruction available as $L'$, so the predictor does not have to spend the single visual token on storing the task specification itself. Thus the bottleneck is placed on dense visual evidence, not on the instruction.
}

Both streams are supervised against the same target with a dual objective combining Mean Squared Error (MSE) and cosine distance, \rev{computed per token}:
%
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{full}} &= \big\| \hat z^{\text{full}}_{t+k} - z^{\text{gt}}_{t+k} \big\|^2 + \big(1 - \cos(\hat z^{\text{full}}_{t+k}, z^{\text{gt}}_{t+k})\big), \label{eq:loss_full} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{int}} &= \big\| \hat z^{\text{int}}_{t+k} - z^{\text{gt}}_{t+k} \big\|^2 + \big(1 - \cos(\hat z^{\text{int}}_{t+k}, z^{\text{gt}}_{t+k})\big). \label{eq:loss_intent}
\end{align}
%
The cosine term provides scale-invariant supervision that aligns the semantic direction of the prediction with the target, while MSE acts as a magnitude regulariser that keeps predictions on the DINOv3 feature manifold.

Following asymmetric siamese designs~\cite{gupta2023siamese}, a consistency loss ties the two streams together:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{cons}} = \alpha \cdot \big\| \hat z^{\text{int}}_{t+k} - \text{sg}\big(\hat z^{\text{full}}_{t+k}\big) \big\|^2, \label{eq:consistency}
\end{equation}
where $\text{sg}(\cdot)$ is the stop-gradient operator and we empirically selected $\alpha=0.3$ to scale the term. \rev{The stop-gradient is placed on the \emph{teacher} side: the bottlenecked forecast is pulled towards the accurate one, while the accurate one is not dragged back towards a deliberately impoverished prediction, which would otherwise collapse both streams onto their average.} The total objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{full}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{int}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{cons}}. \label{eq:total}
\end{equation}

{\color{revcolor}
\noindent\textbf{What each term optimises.}
Because $\theta$ is shared, the losses are coupled through parameters even though the two forward passes read different memories. Eq.~\ref{eq:loss_full} trains the shared decoder blocks to use dense visual memory when it is available. Eq.~\ref{eq:loss_intent} applies supervision to the same decoder blocks and fusion module under the bottlenecked memory, forcing $v'_{\texttt{cls}}$ and $L'$ to carry enough task-conditioned context for future prediction. Eq.~\ref{eq:consistency} sends gradients only through the intent prediction branch because the full prediction is stopped; it distils the dense-memory forecast into the branch that will be used after pre-training. The language backbone is frozen and contributes no gradient path. The coupling that addresses the AE's concern therefore comes from the shared fusion module, the shared query tokens, and the shared predictor weights, as made explicit in Algorithm~\ref{alg:pretrain}.
}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\color{revcolor}
\caption{LARM pre-training step}
\label{alg:pretrain}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $V \gets \texttt{DINOv3}(I_t)$ \Comment{frozen}
\State $L \gets \texttt{Proj}\big(\texttt{DistilBERT}(c)\big)$ \Comment{frozen encoder}
\State $V', L' \gets \texttt{BiCrossAttn}(V, L)$ \Comment{trainable}
\State $Q \gets \texttt{FutureTokens} + \texttt{PosEmbed}$ \Comment{learned, content-free}
\State $z^{\text{gt}}_{t+k} \gets \texttt{sg}\big(\texttt{DINOv3}(I_{t+k})\big)$ \Comment{frozen teacher}
\State $\hat z^{\text{full}}_{t+k} \gets F_\theta\big(Q \mid [v'_{\texttt{cls}}, v'_{1:N}, L']\big)$
\State $\hat z^{\text{int}}_{t+k} \gets F_\theta\big(Q \mid [v'_{\texttt{cls}}, L']\big)$ \Comment{same $\theta$, same $Q$}
\State $\mathcal{L} \gets \mathcal{L}_{\text{full}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{int}} + \alpha\big\|\hat z^{\text{int}}_{t+k} - \texttt{sg}(\hat z^{\text{full}}_{t+k})\big\|^2$
\State update $\{\texttt{Proj}, \texttt{BiCrossAttn}, F_\theta, Q\}$ by $\nabla\mathcal{L}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Residual-guided policy learning}
\label{subsec:policy_learning}

Following pre-training, we adapt the learned representations for downstream manipulation. Existing approaches~\cite{black2023zero,zhu2025lava} condition policies on fully reconstructed future images, which introduces significant computational overhead and generates redundant static information, e.g.\ an unchanged background. In contrast, \rev{we retain the \emph{intent-aligned} stream. The full-information stream exists only to supply the distillation target of Eq.~\ref{eq:consistency} and is not instantiated after pre-training; what survives is the predictor that has been forced to forecast from task context and has been distilled to agree with the accurate forecast. Writing $\hat z_{t+1} = \hat z^{\text{int}}_{t+1}$ for its output,} we compute a latent residual
%
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_z = \hat z_{t+1} - z_t \label{eq:residual},
\end{equation}
%
\rev{where $z_t$ are the frozen DINOv3 tokens of the current observation, i.e.\ the same space in which the prediction target was defined.}
The residual $\Delta_z$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:residual} represents the minimal update between current and future states. In other words, $\Delta_z$ is a directional cue that highlights what should change to achieve the task, rather than a full future state from which the policy network would have to implicitly recover the required motion.

To translate this latent residual into motor commands, we condition the policy on both the current state $z_t$ and the predicted change $\Delta_z$. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:acdino}, $\Delta_z$ is first fused with $z_t$ via SNR-enhanced normalisation. This operation amplifies the $\Delta_z$ signal, ensuring that it is not disregarded as background noise by the policy model, and produces a combined representation $z_{fused} = \text{Norm}(z_t \oplus \Delta_z)$. This representation is then concatenated with proprioceptive joint states $q_t$ and fed to the policy network $\pi(a_t \mid z_{fused}, q_t)$, which outputs actions via standard behavioural cloning objectives.

{\color{revcolor}
\subsection{Deployment}
\label{subsec:deployment}

The predictor operates \emph{in the control loop}. At every control step the current RGB observation and the instruction are encoded, fused, and passed once through the intent-aligned stream; the residual is always computed from the observation the robot is currently seeing, so the guidance signal is re-estimated as the scene evolves. The predictor is never rolled out over multiple steps and is never executed once per episode. Note also that $k$ is a property of pre-training on web video and not a control horizon: $\Delta_z$ is consumed as a direction, and its magnitude and time scale are absorbed by the action decoder, which is trained against real actions.

The retained predictor emits a sequence of $N{+}1$ tokens in the frozen DINOv3 feature space, aligned one-to-one with the teacher tokens: index $0$ is the predicted future \texttt{[CLS]} and indices $1{:}N$ are the predicted future patches. Eq.~\ref{eq:residual} is therefore applied token-wise, and $\Delta_z$ retains spatial structure. This single output supports both action spaces we consider, with different read-outs. For \textit{1-D continuous control} that predicts joint angles or end-effector poses~\cite{assran2025v}, we take the \texttt{[CLS]} residual as a $d$-dimensional vector and concatenate it with $z_t$ and $q_t$ as input to a multi-layer perceptron policy. For \textit{2-D spatial-action policies} designed for vision-based affordance models (e.g.\ tabletop pick-and-place~\cite{shridhar2022cliport}), we reshape the $N$ patch residuals into a $\sqrt{N}\times\sqrt{N}\times d$ spatial map and fuse it with the policy's visual backbone via convolutional layers, providing an explicit prior on where and how to interact. In both cases the predictor is unchanged; only the read-out differs, which allows LARM to integrate with low-dimensional state policies and high-dimensional visuomotor architectures without architectural modification.
}
