\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
We evaluate TRAIL through representation analysis, simulated control, real-robot manipulation, and ablation. We first inspect language grounding after human-video pre-training, then measure downstream control on Franka Kitchen, Meta-World MT10, and a physical UR5 setup, and finally isolate the contribution of the retained latent residual.


\subsection{Analysis of pre-trained representations}
\label{sec:exp_visualization}
We first validate whether TRAIL successfully learns to extract task-relevant dynamics during pre-training.

\noindent\textbf{Pre-training details.} 
We pre-train TRAIL on human-centric videos using the Kinetics-400 training split~\cite{kay2017kinetics, carreira2017quo}, without any in-domain robotic manipulation data. To construct the dataset for our prediction-based self-supervised learning, we sample pairs of frames from each video. Specifically, we select the $1^{\text{st}}$ frame as the initial observation and the $96^{\text{th}}$ frame as the target future state. This specific temporal gap ($k=96$) is chosen to capture meaningful, long-horizon state transitions rather than trivial, high-frequency motions~\cite{kim2025token}. Kinetics-400 is stored at $25$--$30$\,fps, so $k=96$ spans roughly $3$--$4$ seconds of human activity. The gap is large enough so that the transition is not dominated by encoding noise. $k$ is a hyper-parameter of pre-training on web video and \emph{not} a control horizon, i.e. at deployment the predictor is executed at every control step and its output is consumed as a direction, with magnitude and time scale by the action decoder (Sec.~\ref{subsec:policy_learning}).
We adopt DINOv3 (ViT-Base/16)~\cite{simeoni2025dinov3} as our foundational visual backbone, and DistilBERT-Base-Uncased~\cite{sanh2019distilbert} as the text backbone. To provide stable and high-quality latent targets without pixel-level reconstruction, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ab_probe}, we employ self-distillation on our visual backbone and formalize a frozen, pre-trained DINOv3 model as a  \textit{Teacher} model~\cite{grill2020bootstrap, caron2021emerging}. 
% Simultaneously, our trainable TRAIL modules process the initial frame with the language instruction to predict this latent future and optimize the loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}$ defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:total}.

\subsection{Simulation results on Franka Kitchen}
\label{sec:exp_franka}
We validate whether dynamics learned from human videos supports long-horizon robot manipulation across five visuomotor tasks with a simulated 9-DoF Franka Emika arm~\cite{franka_kitchen}.




\noindent\textbf{Setup.}
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:franka}, we compare against three categories of baselines: standard visual representation models, vision-only predictive models, and vision-language predictive models. All methods use the same downstream behavioral cloning (BC) architecture with frozen visual backbones, so the policy-learning protocol is held fixed while the representation and its pre-training data differ. We report average success rates over 25 demonstrations across the five tasks, two viewpoints, and three random seeds. Table~\ref{tab:dif} separately summarizes the robot-data and annotation requirements of the closest predictive baselines.

TRAIL achieves the highest average success rate on Franka Kitchen as shown in Table~\ref{tab:franka}. It improves over strong frozen-representation baselines such as MVP~\cite{radosavovic2023real} (71.7\%) and Ego4D-trained R3M~\cite{nair2022r3m} (69.5\%) by +9.5 and +11.7 percentage points, respectively. Even without robot-video pre-training data, the intent stream learned from human videos transfers to an out-of-domain robotic arm and produces a representation useful for closed-loop control.
Compared with recent vision-language predictive models such as LaVA-Man~\cite{zhu2025lava} (79.2\%) and MPI~\cite{zeng2024learning} (78.9\%), TRAIL is competitive on the average and is strongest on several complex articulation tasks. For example, TRAIL reaches {62.0\%} on ``Open microwave'' and {95.7\%} on ``Flip switch,'' outperforming all predictive baselines on these tasks. 
These results show that our approach to explicitly modeling task-relevant transition directions can be effective.

\subsection{Multi-task generalization on Meta-World}
\label{sec:exp_metaworld}
Franka Kitchen evaluates five tasks that share a single kitchen scene and a common arm configuration. To test whether the task-relevant residual also helps when a \emph{single} policy must cover heterogeneous scenes, objects and motion primitives, we additionally evaluate on Meta-World MT10~\cite{yu2020meta}, a ten-task multi-task benchmark on a simulated Sawyer arm.

\noindent\textbf{Setup.}
We collect 100 expert demonstrations per task with the benchmark's scripted policies and train a single language-conditioned multi-task policy by offline behavioral cloning, with no reinforcement learning and no online interaction. As in Sec.~\ref{sec:exp_franka}, every backbone is kept frozen and only the action decoder is trained. All methods, including ours, use the identical pooled action decoder, so the only quantity that differs across rows of Table~\ref{tab:metaworld} is the representation supplied to it. We report mean closed-loop success over three random seeds, evaluating 50 episodes per task and seed. All methods use the same demonstration splits, random seeds, action decoder, camera and proprioceptive inputs, and evaluation configurations. The evaluation seeds are disjoint from all demonstration-collection seeds, preventing policies from being scored on object configurations seen during data collection.

\noindent\textbf{Visual input.} We render the \texttt{corner2} third-person camera at $224\times224$. The policy consumes a single RGB frame together with a $4$-dimensional proprioceptive state (end-effector position and gripper aperture); no wrist camera, no depth channel and no frame stacking are used. Object positions are randomised by the benchmark at every reset, and the evaluation seeds control that randomisation.

TRAIL reaches a \textbf{78.2\%} MT10 average, ahead of the strongest baseline R3M~\cite{nair2022r3m} (70.0\%). The aggregate margin is modest, but it is not spread evenly across the suite, and the per-task pattern is the informative part. Six of the ten tasks --- Door, both Drawer tasks, Button and Window-Close --- are at or near saturation for every method that yields a usable representation, and therefore carry no discriminative signal. The entire gap comes from two tasks: ``Pick-Place'' (\textbf{40.0\%} vs.\ at most 18.0\% for any baseline) and ``Push'' (\textbf{70.0\%} vs.\ at most 38.0\%).

Fig.~\ref{fig:failure_stages} further decomposes Push and Pick-Place outcomes by the furthest stage reached. TRAIL reduces failures before object contact and shifts the remaining Pick-Place failures toward the later placement stage.

These two tasks share a property that the saturated ones lack: the target object moves while the gripper approaches it, so the correct action depends on where the scene is going rather than only on where it currently is. A static embedding is sufficient to replay a memorised opening trajectory, but it under-determines the action once the goal is non-stationary. Conditioning on the task-relevant residual $\Delta_z$ supplies exactly the missing directional signal, which is why the benefit is invisible on the articulation tasks and decisive on the two dynamic ones. Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation} isolates this effect by removing $\Delta_z$ from our own policy rather than comparing across representations.

The baseline ordering also differs from Franka Kitchen in an informative way. VC-1~\cite{majumdar2023we} (38.8\%), a masked-autoencoding model, transfers markedly worse than the contrastively pre-trained R3M despite a larger backbone. Under our frozen-backbone, shallow-decoder protocol, this result suggests that reconstruction-oriented features are less linearly separable for these control tasks.

\subsection{Real-robot experiment}
\label{sec:real_robot_tabletop}
    % To further assess the robustness of our learned representations and their sim-to-real transferability, we deploy TRAIL on a physical UR5 robotic arm. Real-world environments introduce severe challenges for representation learning, including varying lighting conditions, complex visual distractors, and partial occlusions—factors that frequently degrade the performance of policies relying on standard static visual features.


We further validate TRAIL on a physical UR5 setup where language-conditioned control must handle object appearance shifts, clutter, and contact-rich motions.


\noindent\textbf{Setup.} The hardware setup includes a UR5 robotic arm with a wrist-mounted Intel RealSense camera observing a $60 \text{ cm} \times 30 \text{ cm}$ workspace (Fig.~\ref{fig:setup}). The policy uses RGB images only; depth measurements are not used. We collect 200 language-annotated expert demonstrations and train each method under the same multi-task protocol, comparing TRAIL with CLIPort~\cite{shridhar2022cliport}, MPI~\cite{zeng2024learning}, and LaVA-Man~\cite{zhu2025lava} using identical demonstrations and task instructions. Each trained policy must select actions conditioned on the language command rather than on a task ID alone. To test zero-shot generalization, five colored blocks and five novel objects are strictly excluded from the training data and introduced only at evaluation time. We evaluate seven subtasks: stacking blocks, packing blocks/objects, packing unseen blocks/objects, pushing piles, and pressing buttons. For each subtask, we conduct 15 independent evaluation trials.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{ieeeconf/figure/setup_cropped.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Real-robot setup}. A wrist-mounted camera provides RGB observations for UR5. Depth is not used.}
    \label{fig:setup}

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:robot_results}, TRAIL consistently outperforms the baselines in real-world multi-task manipulation. It achieves an overall success rate of 90\%, substantially higher than the strongest baseline, LaVA-Man ($\sim$80\%), and well above CLIPort and MPI ($\sim$55\%). The strongest evidence appears in the held-out settings: TRAIL reaches 93\% success on ``Packing unseen objects'' (vs.\ 73\% for LaVA-Man) and 80\% on ``Packing unseen blocks'' (a 20-point absolute improvement over LaVA-Man). On the contact-rich ``Pushing piles'' task, TRAIL also reaches 80\%, substantially improves the performance of MPI and LaVA-Man. Together with the affordance visualizations in Fig.~\ref{fig:affordance_maps}, these results support the claim that learning task-relevant latent changes improves robust real-world language-conditioned control and reduces overfitting to the training objects.

\subsection{Ablation study}
\label{sec:ablation}
A core contribution of TRAIL is to retain the intent stream instead of discarding the predictive head after pre-training~\cite{he2022masked, nair2022r3m}. We isolate this design choice by comparing policy inputs that keep either the current state, the predicted future state, the absolute pair, or the residual direction.

We compare four variants:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TRAIL (Encoder-Only):} The downstream policy is conditioned only on static current visual features $z_t$.
    \item \textbf{TRAIL (Predictor-Only):} The policy is conditioned exclusively on the predicted future latent state $\hat{z}^{\mathrm{int}}_{t+k}$ generated by the predictor.
    \item \textbf{TRAIL (Absolute pair):} The policy receives both $z_t$ and $\hat{z}^{\mathrm{int}}_{t+k}$, but the action decoder must infer their difference implicitly.
    \item \textbf{TRAIL (Full):} The policy is conditioned on both the current state and the explicitly computed task-relevant latent residual: $\pi(z_t, \Delta_z)$.
\end{itemize}


\input{ieeeconf/tables/abl}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation_residual} shows that future information is useful, but the residual form is the most effective way to expose it to the policy. On Franka Kitchen, \textit{Encoder-Only} reaches 74.5\% average success but remains weak on ``Open door'' (46.0\%) and ``Open microwave'' (56.3\%). Supplying only the predicted future state improves little on Franka (75.5\%), and giving the absolute pair $(z_t,\hat{z}^{\mathrm{int}}_{t+k})$ is still below the full residual representation (76.1\% vs.\ 81.2\%). The same pattern appears in the MT10 ablation column, where the full model obtains the best average among the four variants. These results show that the gain is not merely from adding a future feature, but from making the task-relevant transition $\Delta_z$ explicit so that the downstream decoder receives both the current state and a direction of task progress.
