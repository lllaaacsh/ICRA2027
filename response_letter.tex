{\Large\bfseries Response to Reviewers}\\[4pt]
{\normalsize Manuscript: \emph{Learning Task-Relevant Latent Residuals for Robot Manipulation}}\\[2pt]
{\normalsize (previously submitted as \emph{LARM}, submission 2433; the method is now named \emph{Trail})}
\end{center}

\vspace{6pt}
\noindent
We thank the Associate Editor and the reviewers for their careful reading. The central
criticism --- that the method could not be reconstructed from the text --- was well
founded, and it led us to find a genuine inconsistency: the paper stated that the
intent stream was discarded and the full-information stream retained, whereas the
opposite is the case. The Method section has been rewritten around this correction.
The main changes are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=4pt]
  \item The two streams are now presented as \emph{one} shared predictor evaluated
        under two memory-access conditions, with an explicit gradient-flow paragraph
        and a pre-training algorithm.
  \item The stream retained at deployment is corrected, and a Deployment subsection
        specifies the output structure, the read-outs, and the execution frequency.
  \item Meta-World MT10 is added as a second simulation benchmark; Franka Kitchen and
        MT10 results are now averaged over three random seeds.
  \item Figures 2 and 3 are redrawn; the related-work and introduction wording flagged
        by Reviewer 9 is revised.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{2pt}
\noindent
Below we list each comment with the corresponding revision.

\section{Associate Editor}

\begin{qa}

\item \C{The main weakness is a lack of clarity.}
\R{Section III has been reorganised into Overview, Latent Space, Language-Grounded
Encoding, Asymmetric Prediction, Training Objective and Gradient Flow, Residual-Guided
Policy Learning, and Deployment, so that the mechanics no longer have to be inferred.}

\item \C{If $F_{\text{intent}}$ is discarded after training, no loss affecting
$F_{\text{full}}$ depends on it, so the training loop is unclear.}
\R{The two streams are not two networks. They are the same predictor $P$ with the same
learned queries $Q$, evaluated with two different memory sets (Eqs.~1--2). All three
losses therefore update the same shared parameters --- the language projection, the
fusion module, $Q$, and $P$. This is stated in Sec.~III-E and shown in Algorithm~1.}

\item \C{The stop-gradient in Eq.~5 may be on the wrong side.}
\R{The reviewer's inference was correct given what the paper said; the error was in the
sentence describing deployment, not in the equation. The full-information stream is the
\emph{teacher} and the intent stream the \emph{student}, and it is the \emph{intent}
stream that is retained at deployment. With that corrected, the stop-gradient belongs
on the teacher side as written. Sec.~III-F and the Fig.~2 caption now state which
stream survives pre-training.}

\item \C{There may also be coupling via the text backbone.}
\R{There is not: DistilBERT is frozen and used under no-grad, so it carries no gradient
path. The coupling is entirely through the shared language projection, fusion module,
queries, and predictor. This is now stated in Sec.~III-E and marked in Fig.~2.}

\item \C{The video is a good teaser.}
\R{Thank you. We have kept it and added the playback-speed and task labels requested by
Reviewer 9.}

\end{qa}

\section{Reviewer 8}

\begin{qa}

\item \C{Clarify the similarities, differences and advantages relative to latent action
training, and explain how dynamics are learned without training latent actions.}
\R{We have added Sec.~II-C, ``Relation to latent action models'', which discusses both
references the reviewer gives, together with the earlier work they build on. In short: we
do not learn an action variable at all. Latent action methods constrain the \emph{action}
variable $u_t$ with an unsupervised bottleneck and then need an alignment stage to map
$u_t$ to real commands; Trail instead constrains \emph{visual memory access}, and
$\Delta_z$ is an observation-space residual consumed directly as policy conditioning.
Dynamics are supervised by the future-latent prediction objective itself, so $\Delta_z$
is the $k$-step transition the instruction implies; the downstream policy is trained
with real action labels, so the residual only has to indicate where the scene should go,
not which command produces it. We also state the cost: because $\Delta_z$ is not an
action it cannot be rolled out for planning, which latent action models can do.}

\item \C{Franka Kitchen is too simple; more simulation results are needed.}
\R{We have added Meta-World MT10 (Sec.~IV-C, Table~II): ten tasks with distinct scenes,
objects and motion primitives, covered by a \emph{single} language-conditioned policy
trained by offline behavioural cloning. All backbones are frozen and every method uses
the identical pooled action decoder, so the representation is the only variable. Results
are averaged over three random seeds with 50 episodes per task and seed. Franka Kitchen
is also now reported over three seeds and two viewpoints.}

\item \C{$k=96$ is too long a horizon for precise manipulation.}
\R{$k$ is a pre-training gap over web video, not a control horizon. Kinetics-400 is
stored at 25--30\,fps, so $k=96$ spans roughly 3--4 seconds of human activity; a much
smaller gap makes consecutive frames near-identical under a frozen DINOv3 encoder and
degenerates the target towards an identity map. At deployment the predictor is executed
at \emph{every control step} and $\Delta_z$ is consumed as a direction, with magnitude
and time scale absorbed by the action decoder. Clarified in Sec.~IV-A and Sec.~III-G.}

\end{qa}

\section{Reviewer 9}

\begin{qa}

\item \C{The \texttt{[CLS]} notation is ambiguous about which modality it comes from.}
\R{Sec.~III-B now states that \texttt{[CLS]} refers \emph{only} to the visual class
token, and that the language encoder's special tokens are never used as separate inputs.
The notation $v'_{\texttt{cls}}$, $v'_{1:N}$, $L'$ is used consistently thereafter.}

\item \C{It is unclear what output structure the retained predictor produces at control
time, and how the \texttt{[CLS]} token used for 1-D control is obtained.}
\R{The retained stream is the \emph{intent} stream (the earlier statement was
incorrect). It emits one future class token and $N$ future patch tokens, aligned
one-to-one with the frozen teacher, so Eq.~7 is applied token-wise and $\Delta_z$ keeps
its spatial structure. 1-D control uses the class-token residual; 2-D spatial-action
policies reshape the patch residuals into a spatial map. The predictor is identical in
both cases; only the read-out differs (Sec.~III-G).}

\item \C{Figure 2 is hard to follow: the text backbone is not marked frozen or
trainable, and the information flow through the bidirectional attention is not shown.}
\R{Figure~2 has been redrawn. Frozen modules (visual backbone, teacher, text backbone)
are marked with snowflakes and all remaining modules are trained; the bidirectional
fusion now shows its two directed updates; and the caption terminology
(intent stream, full-information stream, stop-gradient, retained stream) appears in the
figure itself.}

\item \C{Eq.~(4) uses $V'_{\text{cls}}$ and $L'$, but the figure appears to feed only
the CLS token.}
\R{The figure and the equations now describe the same thing. The bottleneck is a
\emph{memory-access} condition on a shared predictor: the full stream reads
$[v'_{\texttt{cls}}, v'_{1:N}, L']$ and the intent stream reads $[v'_{\texttt{cls}}, L']$
(Eqs.~1--2). Spatial resolution comes from the $N{+}1$ learned queries, not from
decompressing the class token, so the bottlenecked stream still emits a patch-level
forecast.}

\item \C{The visual inputs and camera viewpoints are not specified in either setting.}
\R{Added. Meta-World: the \texttt{corner2} third-person camera at $224\times224$, a
single RGB frame plus a 4-D proprioceptive state, no wrist camera, no depth, no frame
stacking (Sec.~IV-C). Real robot: a wrist-mounted Intel RealSense providing RGB only,
depth unused (Sec.~IV-E and the Fig.~6 caption).}

\item \C{How is occlusion handled in the real-robot setup?}
\R{\pending{describe when the observation is taken relative to the motion --- e.g.\ the
arm returns to a fixed observation pose before each decision, so the placement region is
resolved from an unoccluded view --- and how occlusion-induced failures are counted.}}

\item \C{When and how often is the predictor executed during control?}
\R{At every control step, in closed loop. The robot observes, encodes, runs the retained
intent stream once, computes $\Delta_z$, and predicts an action; the cycle repeats after
the robot moves. The predictor is never rolled out over multiple future steps and is
never executed only once per episode (Sec.~III-G).}

\item \C{Scene initialisation is underspecified, and five trials per task are
insufficient.}
\R{For simulation we now report three random seeds with 50 episodes per task and seed,
using evaluation seeds disjoint from all demonstration-collection seeds, and we state
that object positions are randomised by the benchmark at every reset.
\pending{real-robot protocol: how object positions and orientations are sampled, whether
all methods see the same initial configurations, and the increased number of trials per
task with confidence intervals.}}

\item \C{Minor: the affordance visualisation in Fig.~3 looks odd.}
\R{\pending{redraw: overlay the map on the RGB observation with a perceptually uniform
colormap, add a colorbar and the instruction text, mark the argmax, and state the
normalisation used.}}

\item \C{Minor: citing R3M in support of ``existing models discard the predictive
decoder'' is inappropriate.}
\R{We agree and have rewritten the sentence. The introduction now separates the two
cases: predictive decoders are removed before downstream learning in masked- and
future-prediction pipelines, whereas objectives such as R3M's time-contrastive learning
leave temporal information only implicitly inside a static embedding. R3M is no longer
cited as an instance of discarding a decoder.}

\item \C{The video demonstration is too fast; please indicate the playback speed.}
\R{\pending{label each clip with its playback speed and state in the supplement which
segments are real time.}}

\end{qa}
